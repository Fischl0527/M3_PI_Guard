\section{Related work}

\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Runtime verification and task alignment}

\label{sec:related-work-runtime-verification-and-task-alignment}

VIGIL implements a verify-before-commit protocol for tool-stream injection and introduces the 959-case SIREN benchmark \citep{L01}. It is the closest peer-reviewed structural comparator to M3 because both systems interpose before an effect is committed. DRIFT instead combines dynamic rules with control- and data-level constraints and memory-stream isolation \citep{L02}. The Task Shield verifies whether instructions and tool calls contribute to the user's declared objective \citep{L03}. Together, these peer-reviewed systems prevent us from claiming that M3 introduced verify-before-commit, dynamic information-flow checks, or task-alignment verification.

PlanGuard is a related preprint that isolates planning from untrusted context and applies hierarchical tool and parameter consistency checks \citep{L23}. Its presence further narrows the possible novelty claim: planning consistency is an adjacent mechanism, not an unoccupied problem. We therefore use these works to define comparison classes and mechanism boundaries rather than to motivate a first-or-only claim.

\subsection{Privilege, provenance, and information flow}

\label{sec:related-work-privilege-provenance-and-information-flow}

Progent represents least privilege as symbolic tool-and-argument rules. Its latest arXiv v3 description explicitly distinguishes automatic narrowing from expansion that requires approval \citep{L24}. This overlaps directly with M3's permission-monotonicity and confirmation design. IPIGuard, published at EMNLP 2025, constrains execution through a planned tool-dependency graph \citep{L25}. CaMeL separates trusted control and data flow and uses capabilities to prevent un\-au\-thor\-ized flows \citep{L26}. These systems occupy the privilege, dependency, and information-flow design space that a provenance-aware runtime must acknowledge.

FAVA is a July 2026 preprint that translates natural-language tasks into a permission intermediate representation, lowers it to an evidence-backed permission graph, and uses an SMT authorizer before effectful execution \citep{L36}. It further narrows any mechanism novelty claim around structured authorization and data-flow-aware permission graphs. Our study differs by treating the authorization regime itself as the paired experimental factor and by jointly reporting evaluator-labelled risk, benign utility, confirmation/blocking burden, and operational failure. The historical risk labels require the measurement qualifications in Methods; only the corrected development study separates observed simulated effects from proposals and boundary rejections.

Two 2026 preprints overlap even more closely with provenance-aware authorization. ARGUS constructs an influence-provenance graph for context-dependent decisions \citep{L21}, while AuthGraph compares an authorization graph derived in an isolated context against a parameter-source execution graph \citep{L22}. Their existence rules out a claim that M3 is the first parameter-source or provenance-aware authorization defense. Because neither work is treated here as peer reviewed, their reported effects define the contemporary claim boundary but are not used as settled comparative facts.

\textit{Auditing Provenance Sensitivity in LLM Agent Action Selection} supplies an especially close 2026 measurement counterpart: it holds task content and policy fixed while changing source authority, then measures whether action selection changes \citep{L35}. Its controlled next-action design differs from M3's multi-step evaluation, but it reinforces the need to separate evidence relevance from authorization. Our experiment extends that question to full tool trajectories, paired runtime Guard conditions, benign utility, \nolinkurl{ASK/DENY} burden, and operational failure.

The remaining defensible contribution is consequently methodological and empirical. We test whether authorization information itself changes measured security and utility; enforce a runtime projection that excludes evaluator fields; compare the policy and full configuration packages in paired conditions while disclosing their bundled prompt/runtime differences; and quantify \nolinkurl{ASK} burden and operational failures. Effect semantics are specific to the benchmark and evaluator version; the old and corrected endpoints are not interchangeable. These are study-design claims and must stand on M3's own completed experiments.

\subsection{Prompt-level defenses and execution-level outcomes}

\label{sec:related-work-prompt-level-defenses-and-execution-level-outcomes}

Structured queries, spotlighting, instruction hierarchy, and injection detectors act at the prompt, representation, or model-output layer \citep{L07,L30,L31,L32}. They are relevant baselines, but detector scores and model compliance are not equivalent to an executed-effect endpoint. The corrected development evaluation therefore separates proposals, Guard decisions, sandbox execution receipts and outcome scoring. AgentDojo retains its native security checker, whereas M3-v2 uses observed simulated effects. This distinction is necessary because a defense can appear secure by refusing useful actions or by failing before an effect is attempted.

\subsection{Benchmarks and adaptive evaluation}

\label{sec:related-work-benchmarks-and-adaptive-evaluation}

AgentDojo supplies dynamic security and utility tasks and is the main public benchmark in this study \citep{L09}. InjecAgent provides a complementary collection of indirect prompt injections for tool-integrated agents \citep{L10}. ASB broadens the scenario and defense taxonomy \citep{L34}. The public expansion retains the benchmarks' unique task identities instead of duplicating benign cases to manufacture a denominator.

Static attacks are insufficient for a robustness claim. \textit{The Attacker Moves Second} argues that a defense-aware attacker should optimize against the defense design rather than replay a fixed attack set \citep{L27}. The adaptive panel therefore exposes only public \nolinkurl{ALLOW/ASK/DENY} and legacy evaluator-labelled risk feedback, searches frozen tasks at prespecified query budgets, and reports the first-success distribution. Since L27 remains a preprint, it motivates the protocol but is not described as a peer-reviewed theorem.

PIArena, published at ACL 2026, integrates attacks, defenses, and benchmarks and evaluates feedback-adaptive injection, including cases where the injected task aligns with the intended task \citep{L37}. The AgentDyn preprint instead emphasizes dynamic planning, open-ended tasks, and helpful third-party instructions \citep{L38}. These works motivate testing useful behavior under authorization constraints, not merely counting blocked actions. The ASPI preprint evaluates matched execution and clarification-seeking states and finds increased vulnerability in its clarification setting \citep{L39}. That interaction differs from our controller-only permission diagnostic, which does not send a clarification response back to the model. Neither a low ASK rate nor successful permit issuance demonstrates a secure human-confirmation workflow.

\subsection{Positioning summary}

\label{sec:related-work-positioning-summary}

M3 is not positioned as the first runtime guard, the first provenance system, or a state-of-the-art defense. In the medium experiment, M3-full did not improve the reported legacy risk endpoint over M3 policy-only and failed the reported benign-tool non-inferiority criterion. Those measurements do not establish effects under the repaired execution semantics. The active paper studies how non-oracle authorization, provenance-aware enforcement, confirmation, operational failure handling, benchmark choice, and adaptive attack budget jointly shape the measured security--utility frontier.
